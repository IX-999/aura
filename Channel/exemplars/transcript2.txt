You've been working for the organization for three years. It doesn't matter how you got here. Maybe you needed money. Maybe you had no choice. Maybe you were ambitious and stupid. The point is, you're in now. And being in means you know too much. You know names, roots, operations, faces you should never have seen. And one day while you're washing blood out of your truck because you had to move a package that was leaking, you decide enough is enough. You're going to leave. You're going to escape. How naive you are.

Your first mistake is thinking you can just quit. You mentally draft your resignation letter like this is some corporate job. Dear boss, for personal reasons. You laugh alone in your apartment. The laugh sounds hollow. You know people don't quit. People disappear or die. There's no middle ground, no severance package, no letter of recommendation. There are only two ways to leave the organization: in a wooden box or in pieces they never find.

You start planning in secret. You can't search for anything online. They have people who monitor. You can't ask anyone. Anyone could be an informant. So, you plan in your head, in the shower, in those moments between falling asleep and waking up when your brain isn't fully alert yet. You need money. You need fake documents. You need a destination where they don't have a presence. Good luck finding that place. They're in 23 Mexican states. They have deals with cartels in Colombia, with the Italian mafia, with Chinese triads. The world is smaller than you think when you're a fugitive from organized crime.

You start stealing small amounts that won't be noticed. 5,000 pesos here, 10,000 there. Not from the organization's money. That would be instant suicide. You steal from your own earnings before they give them to you. From the commissions you're supposed to distribute, you keep a little extra. It's like robbing a shark while you're still swimming in its mouth. In 6 months, you collect 200,000 pesos. It's something. It's not enough. It'll never be enough, but it's what you have.

Your second mistake is trusting someone. His name is Marco. You've been working together for 2 years, moving shipments. He drives, you organize. You seem like friends. You drink beers together, complain about the bosses. One day, after eight beers, you tell him you're tired, that you'd like to get out. Marco nods. We'd all like that, compa. You think it's solidarity. You don't notice how his gaze changes. You don't see how he pulls out his phone when he goes to the bathroom. You don't know you just signed a sentence you can't read yet.

3 days later, they call you to a meeting. It's the lieutenant, second in command. He offers you a cigarette. You don't smoke, but you accept it. How are you feeling? He asks. Stressed, tired? You say you're fine. He smiles. It's just that Marco says you seem off. Says you talk about leaving. Your stomach freezes. You deny everything. I was drunk talking nonsense. The lieutenant nods slowly. Of course, nonsense. But you know what? Take a few days off. Rest. When you leave that meeting, you know the clock has started ticking. You don't know how much time you have, but it's not much.

Marco disappears the following week. His mom puts up posters all over the city. Have you seen Marco Lopez? with a photo where he's smiling at a party. You've seen Marco. You saw him for the last time 9 days ago getting into a black Suburban with three men. You won't see him again. You understand the message. The lieutenant is telling you, "Look what happens to those who talk, even to those who listen."

Now you're afraid to talk even in your sleep. You stop dreaming because dreams can make you say things. You intensify your plans, but now you have to be more careful. You realize they're watching you more closely. Your assigned bodyguard, Raul, is always there now. It's for your safety, they say. You know he's your jailer. He accompanies you to the supermarket, to the gym, to visit your mom. Takes mental note of everything. Who you talk to, how much time you spend in each place. Once you try to leave without him. Where you going, boss? I'll come with you. It's not a question. It's an order disguised as courtesy.

You need fake documents. You know a guy. Everyone knows a guy. This one's called El Chaparro. He makes passports, birth certificates, licenses, works for various organizations. That should be your third warning sign. You pay him 50,000 pesos for a passport with your photo, but a different name. Takes two weeks, he says. In the second week, El Chaparro shows up on the news, executed, two bullets to the head. His workshop burned to the ground. All his files, all his clients, everything destroyed. Coincidence? You don't believe in coincidences anymore. Haven't for 3 years. Someone knows. Someone always knows.

You start noticing things. Your car has a GPS you didn't install. You find it under the driver's seat. You leave it there. Removing it would be admitting you found it. Your phone sounds weird. Echoes, clicks, silences where there shouldn't be any. You check your apartment. You find a small camera behind the living room clock. You don't touch it. Now you live knowing every movement is being watched. You masturbate in the bathroom with the shower running because it's the only place where maybe there aren't cameras. Maybe.

You decide that leaving Mexico isn't enough. You need to disappear completely. Change your identity. Plastic surgery if necessary. You consult internet forums using VPN in internet cafes. You learn about countries without extradition. You learn about witness protection programs in the United States. That last option tempts you. Turn them all in. Testify. Get a new life in Iowa or Montana working at a Home Depot being nobody. But you know the statistics. 15% of protected witnesses end up dead anyway. And you have to live knowing you turned in men whose cousins, brothers, sons will never forget your face.

Your paranoia reaches unsustainable levels. You stop eating at restaurants because any waiter could put something in your food. You stop going to the same oxxo twice. You change your route to work every day. You sleep with a knife under your pillow and a gun in the drawer. You check under your car every morning looking for explosives. Your hands shake constantly. You've lost 18 lbs in 2 months. Your mom asks if you're sick. You tell her it's work stress. You don't know how much of that is true.

You meet someone. Her name is Patricia. She's a dentist, completely removed from your world. For 3 weeks, when you're with her, you pretend you're normal, that you're the logistics manager you said you were. She makes you laugh, makes you forget, makes you feel human. And precisely because of that, you have to end it with her. You leave her with a stupid excuse. I'm not ready for a relationship. She cries. You want to cry, too, but you can't explain to her that you're saving her, that every day she spends with you is one day closer to someone using her as leverage. You've already seen how it works. They kidnap your girlfriend. They send you a finger with the ring you gave her. They tell you to cooperate or the next package is bigger.

You try to get outside help. You go to a lawyer, an expensive one, the kind with an office on the 15th floor with a panoramic view. You explain your situation in vague terms. A client has problems with difficult associates. The lawyer understands immediately. He turns pale. I can't help you. Not with this. He returns the 20,000 pesos you put on his desk as if they were burning. Forget you were here. I'll forget I came. You understand? Even professionals are afraid. The reach of the organization is such that even a lawyer in a glass tower knows there's no real protection.

You consider going to the police, to the federales, to someone. But you know that 40% of municipalities have cops on the payroll. You know there are commanders who have two badges, one official and one from the cartel. You know about operations that got leaked just in time for the bad guys to escape. Once you saw your boss, the lieutenant, having coffee with a state secretary of security. They were laughing, patting each other on the back. When you asked him about it later, the lieutenant said, "Business, kid. It's all business." No, you can't trust the police.

You make one last attempt to get documents. This time, you travel to Tijuana. There's a Korean guy who makes passports that pass any scanner. It costs you 80,000 pesos. Everything you've saved. The passport is perfect. Your new identity is Luis Hernandez. You were born in Veracruz in 1989. You have a complete history. Birth certificate, CURP, RFC. It's beautiful. It's your ticket out. You hide it inside the wall of your closet, behind a photo of your family. You just need the right moment.

But the right moment never comes. They assign you a big mission. A shipment of 500 kilos that has to cross from Culiacan to Tijuana. You're the logistics coordinator. You can't say no. It's a lot of responsibility, says the lieutenant, but we trust you. The word trust sounds like a threat. You know this job isn't an opportunity, it's a test. If you fail, you die. If you succeed, you're more tied down than before.

During the operation, something goes wrong. Not your fault. A checkpoint that shouldn't be there. Federal police who aren't on the payroll. There's a shootout. Three of your men die. The shipment is lost. You return empty-handed with your soul in pieces. You know what's coming. You've seen what they do to people who lose shipments. The lieutenant summons you. Tells you the boss wants to see you.

That night, you don't sleep. You write a letter to your mom that you'll never send. You ask her forgiveness for everything. The meeting with the boss is at a ranch 3 hours outside the city. You arrive expecting the worst. But he's calm. Too calm. I understand it wasn't your fault, he says. These things happen. You feel relieved for a second. Then he continues, but the shipment is worth 15 million pesos. Someone has to pay. He brings out a folder, opens it. Inside are photos of your mom entering the market, leaving mass, at her house. You understand? We need guarantees, says the boss. It's not a question.

Your mom becomes your chain. They don't tell you directly that they'll kill her if you run. They don't need to. The photos are enough. Now you have a bodyguard assigned to her, too. For her protection, they say. Your sister calls you scared. Why are there armed men following mom? You make up an excuse about generic threats. She doesn't believe you, but stops asking. Everyone in the family knows something's wrong. Nobody wants to know exactly what.

You try to accelerate your escape. You can't wait for the perfect moment anymore. You buy a bus ticket to Mexico City with your fake identity. From there, you plan to fly to Colombia, then to Spain. You have 30,000 pesos you stole from a recent operation. It's not much, but it's something. The day before your departure, Raul, your constant shadow, asks you, "Where are we going tomorrow?" Your heart stops. "I have the day off," you say. He smiles. "That's great. Me, too. We can do something together." You understand you don't have days off. You mentally cancel the ticket.

You start taking stupid risks. One night after a delivery, you get drunk at a dive bar in a town nobody knows. You talk to the bartender. You tell him you're trapped, that you want to escape. He listens sympathetically, serves you another drink. We all have chains, friend. You pay and leave. 2 days later, that bartender turns up dead in a dump. Coincidence? Maybe. Probably not. You stop talking to strangers.

You become religious. You were never a believer, but now you go to mass on Sundays. You pray to virgins and saints. You promise that if you get out of this, you'll be a better person. You'll donate to charity, help people. The promises sound hollow even to you. God, if he exists, probably stopped listening to you a long time ago. After the third time you transported something that smelled like death. After the fifth time you saw how they beat someone until they were unrecognizable and you did nothing.

You discover the lieutenant is planning to expand operations to Central America. They need trusted people there. Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua. They offer you the position. It's a promotion, they say. Better pay, more responsibility. It's also farther from Mexico, farther from your mom, from what little remains of your normal life. But maybe, just maybe, it's your opportunity. If you're in Guatemala, you could escape to Belize. From Belize to somewhere in the Caribbean, small, forgotten. You accept the position knowing it's probably a trap, but a trap with a 1% chance of escape is better than a cage with zero.

You move to Guatemala City. The apartment they give you is nice. 10th floor, view of the volcanoes. It also has armed guards in the lobby. Your new bodyguard is a huge Guatemalan named Julio. He doesn't talk much, just follows you. You realize you changed prisons, but you're still imprisoned. You establish the operations, contacts with local gangs, trafficking routes, safe houses. Everything works like a well-oiled machine. Your boss in Mexico is impressed. He congratulates you, promises you a bonus. Every congratulation is one more link in your chain.

One night, Julio gets drunk. It's the first time you've seen him human. He tells you he has a son, that he hasn't seen him in 2 years, that this job pays well but costs everything else. You see a mirror in him, two prisoners watching each other. You ask him, "Have you ever thought about leaving?" He looks at you with eyes that have seen too much. "All the time. But I have a family. They know where my family lives." No need to say more. The conversation dies there.

You meet a gringo in a bar. His name is Robert. Says he's an English teacher. He's probably DEA or CIA or some alphabet soup agency. But he's interested in you. He asks you subtle questions. How long have you been in Guatemala? Who do you work for? You're not an idiot. You know what this is. You could cooperate. You could turn everything in. Testify against the organization, witness protection program, new identity, new life. But you also know the DEA has leaks, that informants have died because someone on the American side talked too much. And you know that if you become a witness, your mom, your sister, your nephews, they all have a life expectancy of approximately 48 hours. You tell Robert you're an accountant, that you live a boring life. He smiles. He doesn't believe you, but doesn't insist. He leaves and you never see him again. But now you have extra paranoia. What if Robert tells someone that a Mexican in Guatemala seems nervous? What if the organization has contacts with the DEA and they find out? Your mind creates infinite conspiracies. You sleep 3 hours that night.

6 months pass in Guatemala. You've abandoned the idea of escaping. Or rather, you've postponed it indefinitely. Now you just survive. Day by day, operation by operation. You've developed stomach ulcers. The doctor says it's stress. Prescribes pills that don't work. You started smoking even though you hated cigarettes. It's something to do with your hands when they shake.

One day, the order comes. The boss wants to see you in Mexico to discuss the expansion. They say it's non-negotiable. You fly back knowing you might not return. On the plane, you write passwords for bank accounts on a piece of paper. You memorize them. You eat it. If something goes wrong, at least they won't have that money. The meeting is cordial. Too cordial. The boss hugs you, calls you son, tells you you're doing excellent work. Then, almost as an afterthought, he mentions, "Your mom looks good. I saw her the other day at the market." Your blood freezes. He's never mentioned your mom before. Not directly. "I sent her flowers," he continues. "Older people deserve respect." It's a threat as clear as if he'd put a gun to your head. He's saying, "I know exactly where your weak points are."

You return to Guatemala destroyed. You know you'll never escape. That this is your life now. You've seen too much. You know too much. You're too useful to be released and too dangerous to be neglected. You're in the perfect limbo. Alive but not free. Valuable but disposable. Necessary but never safe.

You start considering final options. Options you never thought you'd consider. Suicide seems less irrational every day. At least it would be an exit you control. But they've taken even that from you. If you kill yourself, your family pays. "Mental instability can be contagious," the lieutenant once said, referring to someone else who blew his brains out. The suicide's family disappeared two weeks later. Not even your death is yours.

You develop a final escape plan. Crazy, desperate, impossible. During a trip to Costa Rica to establish contacts, you simply disappear. Leave everything. Phone, clothes, money. You walk into the jungle and never come out. You become nobody. Live in remote villages, work on coffee farms for food. You have no name, no past. It's a beautiful fantasy. It's also completely unrealizable because for every step you plan, they've planned three. Because there are people in Costa Rica, too. Because the jungle isn't as big as you think. Because eventually, you'll need a doctor, a dentist, a bank, and that's where they'll find you.

One night you get a call. It's a number you don't know. You answer cautiously. The voice on the other end is distorted, electronic. "We're watching you. We know you want to leave. Don't." They hang up. You throw the phone and stomp on it until it's in pieces. You buy a new one. But the message is clear. Even your thoughts of escape have been detected. The surveillance is total.

Julio disappears one day. He simply doesn't show up for work. His replacement is a younger guy, more serious, more armed. What happened to Julio? You ask. He was relocated, they tell you. But you see in your new guardian's eyes that Julio wasn't relocated. Julio is probably in a hole somewhere. Maybe he tried to escape. Maybe he just knew too much. Maybe his existence became inconvenient. The message is clear. Everyone is replaceable.

5 years after you started planning your escape, you're still there. Older, more tired, more broken. You've abandoned the illusion of freedom. Now you just wait. What are you waiting for? You don't know. Maybe a war between cartels that lets you slip away in the chaos. Maybe for them to kill you quickly instead of slowly. Maybe a miracle where your value to the organization decreases exactly when your dangerousness also decreases, creating a 48 hour window where being murdered isn't profitable and escaping is possible.

But you know the truth. The truth that everyone who has tried to escape eventually accepts. There's no way out. There's only waiting. Waiting for you to make a mistake. Waiting for you to become a risk. Waiting for someone new to come do your job better than you. Waiting for your expiration date to finally arrive.

You're a fugitive who never fled. A prisoner without bars. A man who wanted to escape but discovered the cage has no doors because it never needed them. The chains are invisible but unbreakable. Family, knowledge, fear, constant surveillance.

This is what you learned after years trying to escape. You don't escape from a cartel. You survive until they decide you've stopped being useful. And then you don't escape. You simply end. And maybe if you're lucky, you end quickly. That's the only freedom you have left. The hope for a death that doesn't hurt too much.
